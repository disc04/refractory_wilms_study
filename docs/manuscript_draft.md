# Beyond Histology: Acquired Anaplastic Transformation in Wilms Tumor Engages a Unique NF-κB/EMT Program Distinct from De Novo Anaplastic Disease

**Draft: Design, Methods, Results, and Discussion**

---

## Study Design

### Background

Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells are a rare subpopulation of cancer cells that survive high-dose treatment through non-genetic, reversible adaptations rather than permanent mutations. They are characterised by slow cycling, metabolic plasticity, and high dependency on specific survival pathways, acting as a hidden reservoir for relapse. Anaplastic transformation in Wilms tumor may represent a clinically observable, population-scale analogue of this phenomenon — a shift from therapy-sensitive to therapy-resistant identity that shares key phenotypic features with DTP states, including acquired chemoresistance and the loss of differentiated cellular identity.

Wilms tumor (nephroblastoma) is the most common renal malignancy of childhood, accounting for approximately 5% of all pediatric cancers. Despite overall survival exceeding 90% for localized favorable histology disease (FHWT), a subset of patients with diffuse anaplastic histology (DAWT) face substantially worse outcomes. DAWT is defined by severe nuclear enlargement, atypical mitoses, and near-universal somatic *TP53* mutations, carrying a markedly elevated risk of treatment failure. Wilms tumor recurs in approximately 15–30% of cases, with unfavorable histology conferring the greatest relapse risk.

Here we focus on a small group of patients who initially present as FHWT but undergo anaplastic transformation at relapse — their recurrent specimens are reclassified as anaplastic by histopathology, indicating a shift in cellular differentiation state that was absent at diagnosis. We do not have genomic data for the recurrent specimens, and cannot exclude a mutational contribution to the transformation; the genetic architecture of the AT event itself remains unknown. We explore the transcriptomic landscape of this primary→recurrent transition using matched tumor pairs, asking what gene expression changes accompany the acquisition of anaplastic histology and whether these changes overlap with the transcriptional identity of de novo DAWT or represent a distinct molecular trajectory. We situate this analysis alongside the broader FHWT relapse/progression axis to address whether anaplastic transformation is a molecular extreme of standard relapse or a biologically separate route to treatment failure.


### Cohort Description

The full dataset comprises 136 samples from the TARGET-WT cohort: 124 primary tumors (82 FHWT, 42 DAWT), 6 solid tissue normals, 5 recurrent tumors, and 1 metastatic specimen. Primary tumor stage distribution spans Stage I (n=16), Stage II (n=50), Stage III (n=41 + 4 Stage IIIB), and Stage IV (n=13). The cohort has a slight female predominance (70 female, 54 male among primary tumors). Four patients with histologically confirmed anaplastic transformation — TARGET-50-PAJPDC, TARGET-50-PAJNGH, TARGET-50-PALFME, and TARGET-50-PAJNTJ (all with pathology_subtype = "anaplastic Wilms" in the GDC annotation) — contribute matched primary and recurrent samples to the AT analysis (Step 3) and are excluded from the main preprocessing cohort to avoid confounding the FHWT/DAWT comparison. A fifth recurrent patient (TARGET-50-PALJIP, pathology_subtype = "relapse Wilms") underwent relapse without anaplastic transformation and is retained in the main FHWT cohort. The single metastatic specimen (TARGET-50-PAJLUJ) was excluded from all differential expression analyses.

### Analytical Framework and Design Decisions

Three complementary differential expression analyses were designed to address distinct biological questions along the aggressiveness axis.

**Step 1: Histological identity (DAWT vs FHWT).** The baseline comparison establishes the transcriptional separation between the two major histological classes using 76 FHWT and 39 DAWT primary tumors (after exclusion of QC-flagged samples). FHWT is used as the reference level so that genes upregulated in DAWT (positive LFC) directly define the de novo anaplastic transcriptional program. This convention aligns the Step 1 UP gene set with the Step 3 UP gene set (upregulated in anaplastic recurrence), enabling a direct overlap test of whether acquired anaplasia converges on the same transcriptional identity as de novo DAWT.

**Step 2: Relapse trajectory within FHWT — primary vs secondary resistance.** All specimens in this comparison are primary tumor RNA-seq samples; "Relapse" and "Progression" are retrospective clinical outcome labels. This design answers a prognostic question: do primary FHWT tumors destined for progression already differ transcriptionally at diagnosis from those that will later relapse? Progression (failure to achieve complete remission) represents primary, intrinsic resistance to therapy; relapse (recurrence after complete remission) represents secondary, acquired resistance. The two outcome groups therefore represent distinct failure modes, and differences between their primary tumor transcriptomes reflect biology present before treatment rather than treatment-induced changes. All 4 FHWT progressors are female, whereas the relapse group contains 36 females and 33 males — a perfect sex confound that precludes modelling sex as a covariate. The primary analysis is restricted to female patients (36 relapsers vs 4 progressors, n=40). The full mixed-sex analysis is retained as supplementary. DAWT relapse vs progression is run as exploratory only (n=3 progressors).

**Step 3: Anaplastic transformation (paired longitudinal).** Four patients with histologically confirmed matched primary (FHWT) and recurrent (anaplastic) specimens are analysed using a paired within-patient design, blocking on patient identity to remove inter-individual variance. The paired design (n=8 samples; formula ~patient + time_point) maximises power given the small cohort size, and directly captures molecular changes specific to the primary-to-anaplastic transition. Two overlap analyses are performed: (i) AT recurrent UP genes vs FHWT relapse/progression DEGs (Step 2), testing whether anaplastic transformation is a molecular extreme of standard FHWT relapse; (ii) AT recurrent UP genes vs de novo DAWT UP genes (Step 1), testing whether acquired anaplasia converges on the same transcriptional program as de novo DAWT.

Four samples identified as outliers by within-tumor Pearson correlation (mean *r* < 0.90 against the primary tumor cohort) were assigned a QC flag and excluded from differential expression analyses; they are retained in QC visualisations. All QC-flagged samples are from the primary tumor group (3 DAWT, 1 FHWT).

## Methods

### Data Source, Ingestion, and Preprocessing

RNA-seq count data and clinical metadata were obtained from the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) Wilms Tumor dataset, accessed through the NCI Genomic Data Commons (GDC; project TARGET-WT). Gene expression quantification was produced by GDC using the STAR aligner against the GRCh38 reference genome with GENCODE v36 annotation. Unstranded read counts were used throughout. Clinical and pathological annotations were retrieved from the GDC Clinical Supplement files.

Raw GDC count files containing unstranded read counts were delivered as per-sample TSV outputs from the STAR aligner and further aggregated into a single long-format matrix. For the main cohort (primary tumor samples), only protein_coding genes (GENCODE biotype) were retained, yielding 19,938 unique genes across 125 samples. Counts were pivoted to a genes × samples matrix and duplicates resolved by summing.

Low-abundance genes were removed using the criterion ≥10 counts in ≥10 samples, reducing the gene set to 17,341 expressed protein-coding genes. This filtered gene set was used as the common universe for all downstream analyses, including the anaplastic transformation cohort, ensuring cross-comparison consistency. Normalisation used the DESeq2 median-of-ratios method implemented in `pydeseq2.preprocessing.deseq2_norm`; size factors ranged from 0.329 to 1.549 across the 125-sample cohort. The log2-normalised matrix (log2[normalised counts + 1]) was used for all visualisation and PCA.

For PCA visualisation, a separate normalisation was applied to the full 136-sample matrix (including recurrent/metastatic specimens) to enable projection of all sample types without modifying the main analysis matrices.

For the anaplastic transformation analysis, raw counts for the 8 AT samples were extracted from counts_matrix and restricted to the same 17,341-gene universe.

### Quality Control

Library size distributions and count density curves were generated for all 136 samples. Pearson correlation heatmap was generated using the 130-samples cohort (non-recurrent non-metastatic tumor and normal samples). PCA was fitted on the 130 samples, and all 136 samples were projected onto the first two principal components to enable visual assessment of recurrent and metastatic specimens without biasing the decomposition. Combined group labels (Normal, Primary FHWT, Primary DAWT, Recurrent, Metastatic) were assigned based on sample_type and histology annotations.

Within-tumor Pearson correlation was computed across all 17,341 expressed genes using 124 samples (primary tumor samples only). Outlier detection was applied using a mean pairwise correlation threshold of r < 0.90. Four samples failing this threshold were assigned a QC flag and excluded from differential expression analyses; they are retained in all QC visualizations with an orange ring overlay. All four QC-flagged samples are primary tumors (3 DAWT, 1 FHWT): TARGET-50-PAKGED-01A, TARGET-50-CAAAAM-01A, TARGET-50-PAJMVC-01A, and TARGET-50-PAJNAV-01A.

### Differential Expression Analysis

All differential expression analyses used PyDESeq2, a Python reimplementation of the DESeq2 negative binomial model with Bayesian log fold change shrinkage. Raw integer counts were used as input; samples were provided as columns and genes as rows. The Wald test was used for all comparisons. Log fold changes were shrunk using the `lfc_shrink` method with the appropriate treatment-coded coefficient (format: `factor[T.test_level]`). Genes were classified as significantly differentially expressed using thresholds of |log2FC| > 1.0 and adjusted p-value (Benjamini–Hochberg) < 0.05.

**Step 1 design:** `~histology`, with FHWT as the reference level. Positive LFC indicates higher expression in DAWT (anaplastic program); negative LFC indicates higher expression in FHWT. Input: 76 FHWT and 39 DAWT primary tumors (QC-flagged excluded).

**Step 2 design:** `~first_event`, with Progression as the reference level. Positive LFC indicates higher expression in Relapse; negative LFC indicates a higher expression in Progression. Input: 36 female FHWT relapsers and 4 female FHWT progressors (primary analysis); 69 FHWT relapsers and 4 FHWT progressors of both sexes (supplementary sensitivity analysis).

**Step 3 design:** `~patient + time_point`, with Primary as the reference level for `time_point`. The patient blocking factor (4 levels) removes inter-individual variance, isolating within-patient longitudinal change. Positive LFC indicates upregulation in the anaplastic recurrent specimen. With 8 samples and 5 model parameters (intercept, 3 patient dummies, 1 time_point), the residual degrees of freedom is 3 — very tight; results should be interpreted with caution and LFC shrinkage is essential. Input: 4 matched primary-recurrent pairs from confirmed AT patients.

### Gene Set and Pathway Analysis

A custom Wilms tumor gene panel (`WILMS_PANEL`) comprising 11 known drivers — *WT1*, *CTNNB1*, *WTX*, *TP53*, *SIX1*, *SIX2*, *DROSHA*, *DICER1*, *IGF2*, *MYCN*, *DNMT3A* — was used to contextualise differential expression results in all three analyses. Panel gene log2FC values and significance status are visualised as horizontal bar charts regardless of the |LFC| > 1.0 threshold, to capture biologically relevant sub-threshold changes.

DEG overlap between Step 2 (FHWT relapse/progression) and Step 3 (AT paired) was assessed by merging the significant gene lists on gene name and classifying overlapping genes as concordant (same direction in both comparisons) or discordant (opposing directions).

### Pathway and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Pathway enrichment was performed using gseapy (v1.2.1), a Python implementation wrapping the Enrichr API and the Broad GSEA algorithm. Two complementary approaches were applied.

**Over-representation analysis (ORA).** Hypergeometric test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction was run for six gene sets against four databases: GO Biological Process 2023 (GO:BP), KEGG 2021 (Human), MSigDB Hallmarks 2020, and TRRUST Transcription Factors 2019. The background gene universe was restricted to the 17,341 expressed protein-coding genes from the study cohort. Significance threshold: BH-adjusted P < 0.05. The six ORA gene sets were: (1) AT-specific UP — AT recurrent UP genes excluding the 7 genes shared with the de novo DAWT concordant set (n = 163; this isolates what is uniquely gained in acquired anaplasia, not explained by the DAWT program); (2) AT DOWN — genes suppressed during anaplastic transformation (n = 32; the lost FHWT identity program); (3) de novo DAWT UP — the anaplastic program in primary DAWT tumors (n = 94); (4) FHWT-enriched — genes elevated in favourable histology primary tumors (n = 173); (5) secondary resistance UP — genes elevated in FHWT relapse primary tumors vs progression (n = 101); (6) primary resistance UP — genes elevated in FHWT progression primary tumors vs relapse (n = 52).

**Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA).** Preranked GSEA was applied to three full gene-level rankings (17,341 genes each) using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic with gene permutation (1,000 permutations, seed = 42). The ranking metric was sign(log2FC) × −log10(adjusted P-value), which integrates both effect size direction and statistical confidence into a single monotone score; genes excluded from DESeq2 testing (NA adjusted P-value, n = 0–1,345 depending on comparison) were assigned a score of 0 and ranked in the middle of the list, contributing no directional signal. Preranked GSEA is used as the primary enrichment method for the AT comparison (Step 3) given the small sample size (n = 4 pairs), because it does not require an arbitrary significance threshold and uses information from all tested genes. The three ranked lists were: Step 1 (DAWT vs FHWT; positive = DAWT-enriched), Step 2 (FHWT Relapse vs Progression, female-only; positive = secondary resistance), and Step 3 (AT Recurrent vs Primary; positive = acquired anaplastic program). Gene set size limits: 10–500 genes. Significance: |NES| ≥ 1.5 and FDR q-value < 0.25, following GSEA recommended thresholds for exploratory analyses.

All four databases were used for both ORA and GSEA. GO:BP and KEGG provide broad functional coverage; MSigDB Hallmarks provides 50 curated cancer-biology signatures with low redundancy; TRRUST provides transcription factor–target gene sets, enabling inference of upstream regulatory drivers without requiring direct TF expression data.

### Protein–Protein Interaction Network Construction and Centrality Analysis

Protein interaction data for the 163 AT-specific UP genes were retrieved from the STRING database (v12; taxon 9606 — *Homo sapiens*) via the STRING REST API. Only interactions with a combined confidence score ≥ 700 were retained, corresponding to the "high confidence" tier in the STRING scoring framework; the functional (rather than physical) interaction network was used to capture co-expression, pathway co-membership, and text-mining evidence alongside direct binding. Interactions were retrieved with `add_nodes = 0` to prevent inclusion of non-AT genes that would artificially inflate hub scores. The resulting edge list was assembled into an undirected weighted graph using networkx (v3.x), with interaction scores used as edge weights. Of 163 input genes, 78 formed a connected component with at least one interaction above threshold, yielding a network of 78 nodes and 177 edges. The remaining 85 genes are isolated within this network.

Three centrality metrics were computed for all nodes using networkx: degree centrality (normalised fraction of possible edges), betweenness centrality (fraction of all-pairs shortest paths passing through each node, weighted by interaction score), and eigenvector centrality (recursive measure of connectivity to highly connected neighbours, weighted). Each metric was min–max normalised across the 78 connected nodes. A composite hub score was derived as a weighted average, giving equal emphasis to local connectivity and global bridging capacity while down-weighting the more noise-sensitive eigenvector term. Hub tiers were assigned by quantile thresholding computed on the 78 connected nodes: High (composite score ≥ 70th percentile), Medium (≥ 40th percentile), and Low (below 40th percentile). The full centrality table is provided in Supplementary Table S_ppi_hubs.

### Community Detection

To identify functionally coherent sub-modules within the AT PPI network, Louvain community detection was applied to the weighted graph using `networkx.algorithms.community.louvain_communities()` (seed = 42, resolution = 1.0, weight = "weight"). The Louvain algorithm iteratively maximises the modularity coefficient Q, which measures the density of within-community edges relative to a random null model; Q = 0.474 indicates strong modular organisation. Ten communities were identified in total; the six communities with four or more members were retained for biological annotation and are reported in the main text. Biological annotations were assigned by manual inspection of community member gene functions and cross-referencing with the dominant GSEA Hallmarks and ORA enrichment terms. Community identity was not used as a prior for any statistical analysis; it serves purely as an interpretive framework for network topology. The community assignment for each gene is provided in Supplementary Table S_ppi_communities.

Network visualisations were generated with a community-aware layout algorithm: centroids for each major community were placed on a circle of radius 2.8 in the layout coordinate space, equally spaced by angle; member nodes within each community were positioned using an independent spring layout (`nx.spring_layout`, seed offset per community, scale proportional to community size), then translated to their community centroid. This layout preserves local intra-community structure while ensuring that inter-community spatial separation is visually apparent. A baseline network figure without community detection (spring layout, seed = 42, k = 2.5/√n) was saved separately to enable structural comparison.

### Transcription Factor Regulon Analysis

To distinguish genes in the AT-specific program that are direct transcriptional targets of NF-κB from those upregulated through indirect or paracrine mechanisms, we cross-referenced the 163 AT-specific UP genes against the TRRUST v2 (Transcriptional Regulatory Relationships Unravelled by Sentence-based Text-mining) database, a manually curated compendium of human TF–target relationships. The TRRUST v2 human file (`trrust_rawdata.human.tsv`) was downloaded from the GRNpedia repository (https://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/) and cached locally; a fallback reconstruction from GSEA Step 3 leading-edge gene lists was implemented for offline use.

TF regulon enrichment in the AT-specific gene set was assessed using the one-sided Fisher exact test for all TF regulons with a minimum of three overlapping genes. The background gene set was set to 20,000 protein-coding genes, consistent with the order of magnitude of the human protein-coding transcriptome. Adjusted p-values were computed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure; TFs with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. Results for all tested TFs are provided in Supplementary Table S_tf_all.

A focused analysis was performed for the four TFs identified as primary upstream regulators by ORA and GSEA TRRUST analysis — RELA, NFKB1, JUN, and STAT3 — to characterise the specific partition of direct targets between these regulons. A binary gene × TF membership matrix was constructed for all 165 AT-specific genes, indicating membership in each of the four focal TF regulons. Genes regulated by RELA and/or NFKB1 were classified as canonical NF-κB direct targets; within this set, co-regulation by JUN defined the core canonical+AP-1 tier. JUN-only targets (regulated by JUN but not RELA or NFKB1) were classified separately as AP-1-specific, as their regulation is independent of canonical NF-κB activity. Unique STAT3 targets (regulated by STAT3 but not RELA, NFKB1, or JUN) were classified as a distinct STAT3-only set. The full regulon membership table for all 33 direct targets is provided in Supplementary Table S_tf_regulon.

### Druggability Mapping

Druggability of AT-specific UP gene products was assessed using the ChEMBL database (v34), accessed via the ChEMBL REST API (`ebi.ac.uk/chembl/api/data`). For each of the 163 AT-specific genes, UniProt accession numbers were retrieved via the ChEMBL target search endpoint; mechanism-of-action records, approved drug names, and maximum clinical development phase (max_phase) were extracted for all matched targets. Gene–target mapping was performed by exact HGNC symbol matching against ChEMBL target gene annotations. Targets were classified into druggability tiers based on max_phase: Tier 1 (max_phase = 4, approved drug available), Tier 2 (max_phase 2–3, late clinical candidate), Tier 3 (max_phase 0–1, early clinical or preclinical), and Poorly Drugged (no ChEMBL entry or no clinical-stage mechanism). For genes lacking a direct ChEMBL entry (transcription factors, scaffolding proteins), indirect pharmacological strategies targeting the gene product's binding partners or functional complexes were included where evidence supported their relevance to the AT program. The full druggability table is provided in Supplementary Table S_druggability.


## Results and Discussion

### Cohort Composition and Clinical Overview (Figure 1)

The TARGET-WT cohort comprises 136 samples: 124 primary tumors (82 FHWT, 42 DAWT), 6 solid tissue normals, 5 recurrent tumors, and 1 metastatic specimen. Stage II–III disease predominates (n = 95 of 124 primary tumors). All patients were treated according to the NWTS-5 (National Wilms Tumor Study-5) protocol, providing a uniform treatment background across the cohort. Four patients with histologically confirmed anaplastic transformation contribute matched primary (FHWT) and recurrent (anaplastic) specimen pairs to the AT analysis and are excluded from the main preprocessing cohort to prevent confounding the FHWT/DAWT comparison.

### PCA (Figure 2)


PCA of all 136 samples projected onto the non-recurrent-fitted components reveals the dominant sources of variance in the dataset. PC1 (15.6% variance) primarily separates solid tissue normals from tumor samples, reflecting the fundamental biological distance between normal kidney and nephroblastoma. PC2 (9.0% variance) captures variance within the tumor group, partially separating FHWT from DAWT, though with substantial overlap — consistent with their shared nephroblastic origin and the continuous nature of transcriptional change during anaplastic progression. The five recurrent AT specimens project into the tumor cloud rather than clustering separately, suggesting that the primary-to-anaplastic transition involves focused transcriptional rewiring rather than a wholesale shift in expression state. The four QC-flagged samples are dispersed at the periphery, consistent with their outlier correlation profiles.

### Histological Identity: DAWT vs FHWT (Figure 3)

Differential expression analysis between 76 FHWT and 39 DAWT primary tumors identifies 267 DEGs: 94 upregulated in DAWT (anaplastic program, positive LFC) and 173 upregulated in FHWT (negative LFC), using FHWT as the reference level (|LFC| > 1.0, padj < 0.05). The larger number of FHWT-enriched genes may reflect the greater transcriptional diversity of FHWT, which encompasses multiple histological components (blastemal, stromal, epithelial), while DAWT's anaplastic identity is defined by a more focused set of activated programs.

The de novo DAWT-enriched gene set (94 UP genes) includes *DNTT* (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase — a marker of immature lymphoid/progenitor identity), *SOX15* (a SOX family transcription factor), *MYOCD* (myocardin — muscle and stromal differentiation), and *H1-1* (histone H1.1 — chromatin organisation). Genes enriched in FHWT (173 DOWN genes) include markers of blastemal and early nephronic differentiation, reflecting the predominance of the blastemal histological component in FHWT. This gene set serves as the de novo anaplastic reference for the downstream DAWT∩AT overlap analysis.

Among the Wilms tumor driver panel, *TP53* is the only gene reaching statistical significance (padj = 0.0003), with a modest but consistent decrease in DAWT relative to FHWT (log2FC = −0.56 in the FHWT-reference frame, i.e., slightly lower in DAWT). This sub-threshold fold change most likely reflects lower *TP53* transcript levels in DAWT tumors bearing loss-of-function mutations — a phenomenon consistent with nonsense-mediated decay of mutant alleles or disrupted stoichiometric autoregulation. The remaining panel genes do not meet the |LFC| > 1.0 threshold, consistent with their roles as mutational drivers acting at the DNA level.

### FHWT Primary Tumor Transcriptomics Stratified by Outcome: Primary vs Secondary Resistance (Figure 4)

This analysis compares primary tumor RNA-seq profiles between two FHWT outcome groups: patients who progressed (primary resistance — failure to achieve complete remission; n=4) and those who later relapsed after complete remission (secondary resistance; n=36), restricted to female patients. Because all specimens are primary tumors, these are not measurements of the resistance event itself but of pre-existing transcriptional differences at diagnosis that predict which failure mode a tumor will follow. The analysis asks: are primary and secondary resistance pre-determined at the time of surgery?

153 DEGs are identified (101 upregulated in secondary resistance/relapse, 52 upregulated in primary resistance/progression). The answer appears to be yes — the two outcome groups are transcriptionally distinct in their primary tumors, before any treatment has occurred.

The secondary resistance (relapse) primary tumors are enriched for epithelial differentiation, ECM remodeling, and Wnt pathway markers: *MMP7* (matrix metalloproteinase; Wnt/β-catenin target), *WIF1* (Wnt inhibitory factor-1), *CDH16* (kidney-specific cadherin), and *POU3F3* (Brn-1, nephron-patterning transcription factor). These tumors appear to retain a differentiated, nephron-like identity at diagnosis, suggesting they respond initially to therapy and fail later through a distinct mechanism.

The primary resistance (progression) primary tumors are strikingly immune-enriched: *MZB1* (marginal zone B-cell marker), *SIGLEC1* (CD169, monocyte/macrophage), *IFI6*, *IFI27* (interferon-stimulated genes), and *CFD* (complement factor D) are all elevated in the primary tumors of patients who never achieved complete remission. This immune signature at baseline — before treatment — suggests that primary resistance may involve an immunosuppressive or immune-excluded tumor microenvironment that prevents effective drug delivery or immune clearance from the outset. Whether this represents a pre-existing persister-like niche, consistent with the DTP framework, cannot be determined from bulk RNA-seq alone; single-cell or spatial resolution would be required. Importantly, all Wilms panel driver genes are non-significant, consistent with the shared FHWT mutational background of both groups.

The all-sex analysis (supplementary figures) recovers 114 DEGs but is dominated by Y-chromosome gene artifacts (*USP9Y*, *DDX3Y*, *KDM5D*, *NLGN4Y*), validating the necessity of the female-only restriction.

### Anaplastic Transformation: Paired Primary→Recurrent Analysis (Figure 5)

The paired longitudinal comparison of 4 confirmed FHWT primary and anaplastic recurrent specimens yields 204 DEGs (172 upregulated in recurrent/anaplastic, 32 downregulated). The large imbalance between up- and down-regulated genes indicates that anaplastic transformation is principally an activating transcriptional event, consistent with the gain-of-function nature of *TP53* mutations and the known upregulation of oncogenic programs in anaplastic Wilms. Given the very tight design (n=4 pairs; residual df=3), all results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

The most strongly upregulated genes in the anaplastic recurrent specimens include *CTHRC1* (collagen triple helix repeat containing 1; stromal/fibrotic remodeling), *TNFAIP6* (TNF-stimulated gene 6; inflammatory ECM remodeling), *PTGS1* (COX-1; prostaglandin synthesis and inflammatory signaling), *ITGBL1* (integrin beta-like 1; cell-matrix adhesion), *ABCB1* (MDR1/P-glycoprotein; a canonical multidrug efflux transporter), *IL6* (pro-inflammatory cytokine), and *FOS* (immediate-early response, stress and proliferation). The co-upregulation of *ABCB1* and inflammatory mediators is particularly notable in the context of anaplastic transformation, as it suggests that resistance to chemotherapy (ABCB1-mediated drug efflux) develops alongside an inflammatory reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment — a combination that could explain the notorious treatment refractoriness of anaplastic Wilms after relapse.

Genes downregulated in the anaplastic recurrent specimens include *AGTR1* (angiotensin II receptor type 1), *APELA* (apelin receptor early endogenous ligand), *FBLN5* (fibulin-5; an ECM component associated with epithelial phenotype), *TCF21* (a transcription factor marking stromal progenitors in kidney development), and *HOXB6* (posterior nephron identity). This pattern suggests active suppression of the original FHWT transcriptional identity during transformation, paralleling the loss of differentiation markers observed histologically.

Among the Wilms panel genes, none reach significance at |LFC| > 1.0 and padj < 0.05 in the paired analysis — likely reflecting low power at n=4 pairs rather than a genuine absence of panel gene dysregulation. The sub-threshold behaviour of *TP53* in this comparison should be interpreted cautiously given that all four AT patients carry *TP53* mutations by definition, and bulk transcript levels are not a reliable readout of mutant *TP53* gain-of-function activity.

### Overlap I — AT vs FHWT Relapse/Progression (Figure 6)

The overlap between AT recurrent UP genes (Step 3, n=172) and FHWT relapse/progression DEGs (Step 2, n=153) is minimal: 3 genes overlap, with only 1 concordant (*COL11A1*, upregulated in both secondary resistance/relapse and anaplastic recurrence). This near-complete non-overlap demonstrates that anaplastic transformation does not represent a transcriptional extreme of standard FHWT relapse. The two routes to treatment failure activate largely non-overlapping programs: primary and secondary resistance involve immune microenvironment differences visible at diagnosis, while AT involves post-treatment activation of chemoresistance and inflammatory remodeling pathways. These are biologically distinct failure modes.

### Overlap II — AT Acquired Anaplasia vs de novo DAWT Program (Figure 7)

The overlap between AT recurrent UP genes (n=172) and the de novo DAWT anaplastic program (Step 1 UP genes, n=94) identifies 7 concordant genes upregulated in both contexts: *FOS*, *MMP9*, *EGR1*, *SERPINE1*, *STEAP4*, *COL10A1*, and *SPRR2F*. Two genes are discordant (AT-UP but DAWT-DOWN). The 7 concordant genes share a coherent biological theme: *FOS* and *EGR1* are immediate-early response transcription factors activated by cellular stress, oncogenic signaling, and inflammation; *SERPINE1* (PAI-1) is a marker of aggressive, therapy-resistant phenotypes across multiple cancer types; *MMP9* and *COL10A1* reflect extracellular matrix remodeling shared by both anaplastic states.

The overwhelming majority of AT-UP genes (163/172, 95%) are absent from the de novo DAWT program, demonstrating that acquired and de novo anaplasia are largely independent transcriptional events. They share a common histological endpoint (nuclear anaplasia) and a small stress-response/ECM module, but reach that endpoint through distinct molecular routes. This finding has direct clinical implications: therapeutic strategies developed for de novo DAWT cannot be assumed to address the full transcriptional program of AT tumors, which is predominantly non-overlapping.

### Pathway Enrichment Analysis

#### ORA: AT-specific acquired anaplasia program (Figure 8)

The 163 genes unique to anaplastic transformation (AT-specific UP; AT recurrent UP after exclusion of the 7 DAWT-concordant genes) are dominated by two co-activating programs: epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and NF-κB-driven inflammation. In the MSigDB Hallmarks database, Epithelial Mesenchymal Transition is the most significantly enriched term by a wide margin (OR = 19.2, padj = 4.9 × 10⁻²²), followed by TNF-alpha Signaling via NF-κB (OR = 16.6, padj = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁸) and Inflammatory Response (OR = 9.5, padj = 5.9 × 10⁻⁹). These two programs are not independent: in the context of acquired anaplasia, EMT and inflammatory reprogramming are likely co-regulated facets of the same transition from a differentiated nephron-like identity to a therapy-resistant mesenchymal state.

At the KEGG pathway level, the leading terms are Rheumatoid arthritis (OR = 18.4, padj = 5.4 × 10⁻⁹) and TNF signaling pathway (OR = 13.8, padj = 5.3 × 10⁻⁸), followed by IL-17 signaling pathway (OR = 13.5, padj = 4.2 × 10⁻⁶). These inflammatory pathway annotations collectively reflect the upregulation of cytokines (IL6), prostaglandin mediators (PTGS1/COX-1), and ECM remodeling enzymes (MMP9, CTHRC1) observed in the DE analysis. GO Biological Process terms reinforce this picture: Inflammatory Response (GO:0006954; OR = 7.1, padj = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴), Regulation of Interleukin-8 Production (OR = 13.8, padj = 1.8 × 10⁻⁴), and Cellular Response to Molecule of Bacterial Origin (OR = 10.4, padj = 2.3 × 10⁻⁴) are the top biological process hits, consistent with innate immune pathway activation as part of the acquired anaplastic phenotype.

Upstream transcription factor inference via TRRUST identifies the canonical NF-κB complex as the primary regulatory driver: RELA (p65; OR = 12.5, padj = 4.3 × 10⁻¹⁶) and NFKB1 (p50; OR = 10.6, padj = 4.1 × 10⁻¹³) are the top two hits, followed by JUN (OR = 16.2, padj = 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰) — the AP-1 component that co-activates with NF-κB at shared enhancers. HMGA1 (OR = 64.8, padj = 2.7 × 10⁻⁷) is also significant; HMGA1 is a chromatin architectural protein that promotes EMT and is overexpressed in aggressive pediatric tumors. Taken together, the ORA portrait of AT-specific UP genes describes a tumor that has rewired its transcriptional identity toward NF-κB/AP-1-driven inflammation and EMT — with RELA, NFKB1, and JUN as the principal upstream regulators.

The 32 genes downregulated during anaplastic transformation (AT DOWN) yield a sparser but interpretable enrichment signal. In KEGG, Protein digestion and absorption (OR = 37.1, padj = 4.1 × 10⁻⁵) and ECM-receptor interaction (OR = 30.0, padj = 5.8 × 10⁻⁴) are the top terms, reflecting the loss of collagen-rich extracellular matrix components associated with the original FHWT stromal identity (consistent with the downregulation of FBLN5, AGTR1, and HOXB6 in the DE analysis). No Hallmarks or TRRUST terms reach significance for AT DOWN at n=32 genes, likely reflecting insufficient power to detect set-level enrichment in this small gene list.

#### ORA: De novo DAWT identity and FHWT developmental program (Figure 9)

The de novo DAWT UP gene set (n=94) and FHWT-enriched gene set (n=173) yield divergent but individually sparse enrichment profiles, likely reflecting the smaller gene set sizes relative to the AT-specific analysis.

Among the DAWT UP genes, the top Hallmarks term is KRAS Signaling Dn (OR = 9.1, padj = 1.7 × 10⁻⁴) — the suppression of a KRAS activity signature — which is counterintuitive at first but consistent with the known biology: de novo DAWT is driven by TP53 gain-of-function and chromosomal instability rather than RAS/MAPK hyperactivation, and many RAS target genes are actually expressed at lower levels in anaplastic versus blastemal/epithelial subtypes. TNF-alpha Signaling via NF-κB (OR = 7.2, padj = 1.3 × 10⁻³) and Hypoxia (OR = 5.0, padj = 3.8 × 10⁻²) are also enriched, suggesting that inflammatory NF-κB activation is a shared feature of both de novo and acquired anaplasia — consistent with the 7 concordant AT∩DAWT genes that include EGR1 and SERPINE1, both NF-κB targets. At the KEGG level, Estrogen signaling pathway (OR = 11.9, padj = 6.8 × 10⁻⁴), cAMP signaling pathway (OR = 6.9, padj = 9.6 × 10⁻³), and Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (OR = 5.4, padj = 1.1 × 10⁻²) represent the de novo DAWT program's link to receptor signaling, consistent with the neural/progenitor identity markers (DNTT, SOX15) identified in the DE analysis. TRRUST identifies STAT3 (OR = 14.4, padj = 7.4 × 10⁻³) and the estrogen receptor ESR2 (OR = 37.9, padj = 9.3 × 10⁻³) as candidate upstream regulators of the DAWT-specific program.

The FHWT-enriched gene set (n=173) yields notably sparse enrichment — the only significant Hallmarks term is KRAS Signaling Dn (OR = 7.5, padj = 8.7 × 10⁻⁶), which is also the top DAWT UP hit. This shared enrichment for a KRAS-suppressed state in both histological classes at the ORA level reflects the composition of these gene sets: both DAWT UP and FHWT-enriched contain genes associated with favorable histology developmental programs, and neither class has RAS hyperactivation as a defining feature. At the KEGG level, FHWT-enriched genes are annotated to Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (OR = 4.4, padj = 6.5 × 10⁻³) and Adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes (OR = 5.2, padj = 4.4 × 10⁻²), which likely reflect the calcium-handling and GPCR signaling genes found in the blastemal and stromal components of FHWT. The sparseness of FHWT ORA enrichment overall is consistent with FHWT representing a more heterogeneous, developmentally mixed state that does not cluster tightly around any single canonical pathway signature.

#### ORA: Primary vs secondary resistance (Figure 10)

The two resistance gene sets produce the clearest biological contrast of any ORA comparison. Primary resistance (progression-enriched, n=52 genes) is strongly enriched for immune effector pathway Hallmarks: Allograft Rejection (OR = 9.8, padj = 2.3 × 10⁻³), Complement (OR = 9.6, padj = 2.3 × 10⁻³), Coagulation (OR = 11.8, padj = 2.8 × 10⁻³), Interferon Gamma Response (OR = 7.3, padj = 9.8 × 10⁻³), and Interferon Alpha Response (OR = 11.2, padj = 9.8 × 10⁻³) are all significant. At the KEGG level, Complement and coagulation cascades (OR = 14.6, padj = 4.2 × 10⁻²) and Hematopoietic cell lineage (OR = 12.4, padj = 4.2 × 10⁻²) reinforce a baseline immune/inflammatory microenvironment in primary resistant tumors. TRRUST identifies STAT1 (OR = 42.3, padj = 4.5 × 10⁻³) and EBF1 (OR = 230.5, padj = 4.5 × 10⁻³; a B-lymphocyte lineage TF) as upstream regulators, consistent with the marginal zone B cell and macrophage markers identified in the DE analysis (MZB1, SIGLEC1). These findings confirm and extend the DE-level observation: primary FHWT resistance (failure to achieve complete remission) is associated with an immune-inflamed — but likely immunosuppressive — tumor microenvironment at diagnosis, characterised by complement activation, interferon signaling, and B/myeloid cell infiltration. This immune signature is a pre-existing feature of the tumor rather than a treatment response.

Secondary resistance (relapse-enriched, n=101 genes) shows a strikingly different and much sparser ORA profile: no Hallmarks terms reach significance, and the top GO:BP hits are CNS development (OR = 6.5, padj = 1.8 × 10⁻²) and Negative Regulation of Wnt Signaling Pathway (OR = 8.3, padj = 2.9 × 10⁻²). In TRRUST, LEF1 (OR = 22.9, padj = 2.4 × 10⁻²) is significant — LEF1 is the transcriptional effector of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, consistent with the Wnt-active marker gene expression (MMP7, WIF1) observed in secondary resistance primary tumors. The sparseness of secondary resistance ORA enrichment, contrasted with the dense primary resistance signal, suggests that secondary resistance primary tumors are transcriptionally similar to the broad FHWT background and that their distinct biology may be better captured by GSEA on the full ranked gene list.

#### GSEA: Threshold-free ranked analysis (Figures 11-13)

**Step 1: DAWT vs FHWT.** GSEA on the full FHWT-reference ranked list (positive = FHWT-enriched) confirms and expands the ORA picture. The DAWT-enriched program (negative NES) is dominated by a coherent proliferative cell cycle signature: mTORC1 Signaling (NES = −2.11, FDR < 0.001), E2F Targets (NES = −1.98, FDR < 0.001), MYC Targets V1 (NES = −1.85, FDR < 0.001), G2-M Checkpoint (NES = −1.74, FDR = 0.003), and MYC Targets V2 (NES = −1.50, FDR = 0.022) are all significant. This MYC/E2F/G2M axis identifies unrestrained proliferative drive as the molecular hallmark of de novo anaplasia — consistent with the nuclear enlargement and atypical mitoses that define anaplastic histology. PI3K/AKT/mTOR Signaling (NES = −1.59, FDR = 0.013) and Glycolysis (NES = −1.56, FDR = 0.015) complete the DAWT proliferative-metabolic program. On the FHWT side, Wnt-beta Catenin Signaling (NES = +1.83, FDR = 0.002) is the dominant Hallmarks hit, followed by Hypoxia (NES = +1.76, FDR = 0.003), p53 Pathway (NES = +1.55, FDR = 0.021), and EMT (NES = +1.53, FDR = 0.021). The FHWT enrichment of Wnt signaling is consistent with the known role of CTNNB1 mutations in a subset of FHWT and with the developmental Wnt dependency of blastemal progenitors. In GO:BP, FHWT-enriched terms are centered on vasculogenesis and circulatory system development (NES ≈ 1.96–2.04), reflecting the nephron-associated vascular network in FHWT — consistent with the blastemal and epithelial histological components. TRRUST identifies GATA1 and ETS2/ERG — transcription factors associated with hematopoietic and endothelial lineages — among the FHWT-enriched regulators.

**Step 2: FHWT Relapse vs Progression.** GSEA on the secondary resistance–referenced ranked list (positive = relapse/secondary resistance, negative = progression/primary resistance) produces the most striking separation between the two resistance modes. The secondary resistance program is led by Wnt-beta Catenin Signaling (NES = +1.72, FDR = 0.012) and Glycolysis (NES = +1.71, FDR = 0.007) at the Hallmarks level; in KEGG, Wnt signaling pathway (NES = +2.10, FDR = 0.001) is the top positive hit, with TRRUST identifying LEF1 (NES = +1.85, FDR = 0.019), PAX3 (NES = +1.84, FDR = 0.018), and CTNNB1 (NES = +1.81, FDR = 0.022) as upstream regulators. The primary resistance program (negative NES) is anchored by the same immune hallmarks identified in the ORA: Allograft Rejection (NES = −2.33, FDR < 0.001), Interferon Gamma Response (NES = −2.32, FDR < 0.001), Interferon Alpha Response (NES = −2.21, FDR < 0.001), IL-6/JAK/STAT3 Signaling (NES = −2.01, FDR < 0.001), TNF-alpha Signaling via NF-κB (NES = −1.76, FDR = 0.001), and Complement (NES = −1.62, FDR = 0.007). TRRUST for primary resistance identifies STAT1 (NES = −1.97, FDR = 0.011) and REL (NES = −1.94, FDR = 0.010) — the canonical interferon and NF-κB regulators, respectively — as well as IRF4 (NES = −2.00, FDR = 0.012), a lymphocyte TF consistent with immune infiltration. In KEGG, Staphylococcus aureus infection (NES = −2.57, FDR < 0.001) and Graft-versus-host disease (NES = −2.34, FDR < 0.001) head the primary resistance list — both being proxy gene sets for complement/macrophage/interferon programs rather than their nominal pathogen contexts. The complete absence of any significant secondary resistance enrichment at the Hallmarks level (no Hallmarks FDR < 0.25 in the positive direction), while primary resistance generates 7 significant Hallmarks terms, further underscores the asymmetry: primary resistance is defined by a specific, coherent immune program; secondary resistance is a quieter transcriptional deviation from the FHWT baseline, captured mainly by Wnt and epithelial differentiation signals.

**Step 3: AT Recurrent vs Primary.** GSEA on the AT ranked list (positive = acquired anaplasia, negative = lost FHWT identity) confirms and substantially expands the DE and ORA findings, leveraging all 17,341 genes rather than the 204 DEGs. The acquired anaplastic program at the Hallmarks level encompasses 17 significant positive terms (all FDR < 0.025), forming a unified inflammatory-EMT-resistance program: TNF-alpha Signaling via NF-κB (NES = 1.93, FDR < 0.001), Interferon Gamma Response (NES = 1.83, FDR < 0.001), Inflammatory Response (NES = 1.81, FDR < 0.001), Epithelial Mesenchymal Transition (NES = 1.77, FDR < 0.001), KRAS Signaling Up (NES = 1.77, FDR < 0.001), Allograft Rejection (NES = 1.76, FDR < 0.001), Hypoxia (NES = 1.75, FDR < 0.001), Complement (NES = 1.73, FDR < 0.001), IL-6/JAK/STAT3 Signaling (NES = 1.71, FDR < 0.002), IL-2/STAT5 Signaling (NES = 1.70, FDR < 0.002), Apoptosis (NES = 1.70, FDR < 0.002), Coagulation (NES = 1.68, FDR < 0.003), Interferon Alpha Response (NES = 1.66, FDR < 0.003), and TGF-beta Signaling (NES = 1.51, FDR = 0.022). At the KEGG level, the top enriched terms are TNF signaling pathway (NES = 1.87, FDR < 0.001), IL-17 signaling pathway (NES = 1.86, FDR < 0.001), NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (NES = 1.78, FDR < 0.002), and NF-kappa B signaling pathway (NES = 1.77, FDR < 0.002). TRRUST identifies RELA (NES = 1.87, FDR < 0.001), NFKB1 (NES = 1.86, FDR < 0.001), and JUN (NES = 1.88, FDR < 0.001) as the top upstream regulators of the acquired anaplastic gene program — perfectly concordant with the ORA result. The breadth of this GSEA enrichment, spanning innate immune activation, adaptive immune signaling, EMT, angiogenesis, hypoxia, and coagulation, indicates that anaplastic transformation involves a comprehensive rewiring of tumor biology rather than activation of a single pathway. KRAS Signaling Up appearing prominently here — contrasted with KRAS Signaling Dn enrichment in both FHWT-enriched and DAWT-UP ORA sets — marks an important axis shift: the KRAS effector program is suppressed across the FHWT/DAWT divide (consistent with both histologies being KRAS wild-type) but becomes activated in the acquired anaplastic recurrence, possibly through RAS-independent mechanisms such as NF-κB–MAPK crosstalk or prostaglandin/COX-2 signaling (PTGS1 is among the top AT UP genes). The lost FHWT identity program (negative NES) is captured concisely by Kidney Development (NES = −1.90, FDR = 0.132 — below threshold but biologically noteworthy) and Steroid hormone biosynthesis (NES = −1.89, FDR = 0.039) in KEGG, alongside suppression of the Renin-angiotensin system (NES = −1.75, FDR = 0.123), consistent with the downregulation of AGTR1 and APELA observed in the DE analysis.

#### Cross-comparison: MSigDB Hallmarks heatmap (Figure 14)

The Hallmarks heatmap across all six gene sets reveals a structured axis of cancer biology that maps cleanly onto the clinical aggressiveness spectrum (Figure 6G). Several organising principles emerge.

The MYC/E2F/mTOR proliferative axis is DAWT-specific. In GSEA Step 1, DAWT uniquely enriches for MYC Targets V1/V2, E2F Targets, G2-M Checkpoint, and mTORC1 Signaling. None of these appear as significant positive terms in any other comparison. De novo DAWT identity is therefore defined primarily by hyper-proliferative cell cycle drive — consistent with TP53-mutant, chromosomally unstable tumor biology — rather than by inflammatory reprogramming.

The KRAS axis traverses the entire aggressiveness spectrum but inverts sign. KRAS Signaling Dn is enriched in both FHWT-enriched genes (ORA; padj = 8.7 × 10⁻⁶) and DAWT UP genes (ORA; padj = 1.7 × 10⁻⁴), indicating that suppression of RAS effector outputs is a feature shared by both histological classes in primary tumors. In sharp contrast, KRAS Signaling Up is enriched in the AT recurrent program (GSEA NES = 1.77, FDR < 0.001) — suggesting that anaplastic transformation involves activation of a KRAS-like transcriptional output in a tumor that was KRAS-suppressed at diagnosis. This inversion of the KRAS axis at transformation is one of the most biologically distinctive features of the acquired anaplastic state.

The NF-κB/TNF node links de novo and acquired anaplasia. TNF-alpha Signaling via NF-κB is enriched in both the AT-specific UP set (ORA; padj = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁸) and the DAWT UP set (ORA; padj = 1.3 × 10⁻³), and is the top GSEA hit for AT GSEA Step 3 (NES = 1.93). This shared enrichment identifies NF-κB-driven transcription as the molecular intersection of the two independent routes to anaplastic histology — the only major cancer hallmark that is activated in both de novo DAWT and acquired anaplastic recurrence. Notably, the seven concordant AT∩DAWT genes (FOS, EGR1, SERPINE1, MMP9, COL10A1, STEAP4, SPRR2F) are all downstream of or regulated by NF-κB/AP-1 activity, suggesting that the small overlap between acquired and de novo anaplasia is mediated specifically through this node.

Wnt-beta Catenin Signaling is secondary resistance-specific. It appears only in the secondary resistance/relapse programs (GSEA Steps 1 and 2 positive NES; TRRUST: LEF1, CTNNB1), consistent with the known biology of CTNNB1 mutations in FHWT and the developmental Wnt dependency of blastemal progenitors. Its absence from the DAWT and AT programs confirms that Wnt activity is a marker of the treatment-sensitive, differentiated-relapse phenotype rather than of anaplastic aggressiveness.

The Interferon/Complement/Allograft immune cluster defines primary resistance but unexpectedly also marks the AT recurrent program. In Step 2 GSEA, Interferon Gamma Response (NES = −2.32), Allograft Rejection (NES = −2.33), and Complement (NES = −1.62) are all primary resistance-enriched. These same terms are also significantly enriched in the AT GSEA Step 3 (positive NES = 1.58–1.83). The convergence of an interferon/complement/coagulation immune program in both primary resistance and AT recurrence is unexpected and may reflect a shared feature of therapy-refractory Wilms tumor: an activated innate immune microenvironment that, paradoxically, correlates with failure to respond to or eliminate tumor cells. Whether this represents a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory state, immunosuppressive innate myeloid infiltration, or a tumor-cell-intrinsic mimicry of immune gene expression cannot be resolved from bulk RNA-seq.

## Summary of Results

The analysis establishes three complementary transcriptional portraits of Wilms tumor aggressiveness and three pairwise comparisons between them.

**De novo anaplastic identity (DAWT vs FHWT, Step 1):** 267 DEGs distinguish the two histological classes. Using FHWT as the reference, 94 genes define the de novo anaplastic program (DAWT-enriched); 173 genes are FHWT-enriched. The DAWT UP set includes stress-response and progenitor-associated transcription factors (*DNTT*, *SOX15*) alongside stromal markers (*MYOCD*). *TP53* expression is modestly but significantly reduced in DAWT (padj = 0.0003), consistent with mutant-allele transcript decay. This gene set serves as the reference de novo anaplastic program for the AT convergence analysis.

**Primary vs secondary resistance within FHWT (Step 2):** Comparing primary tumor transcriptomes by retrospective outcome, 153 DEGs distinguish primary resistance (progression; n=4) from secondary resistance (relapse; n=36) — demonstrating that the two failure modes are transcriptionally distinct before treatment begins. Secondary resistance tumors are enriched for Wnt-active, nephron-like programs (*MMP7*, *WIF1*, *CDH16*, *POU3F3*); primary resistance tumors show elevated immune microenvironment markers (*MZB1*, *SIGLEC1*, *IFI6/27*, *CFD*) at diagnosis, suggesting an immune-excluded or immunosuppressive baseline consistent with pre-existing therapy resistance. A sex confound (all 4 progressors female, 33/69 relapsers male) was detected and corrected; the primary analysis uses female patients only.

**Anaplastic transformation (Step 3):** The paired Primary→Recurrent analysis (n=4 pairs; exploratory) yields 204 DEGs, overwhelmingly upregulated in the anaplastic recurrence (172/204). The transformation program is dominated by chemoresistance (*ABCB1*/MDR1), inflammatory ECM remodeling (*TNFAIP6*, *CTHRC1*), and inflammatory mediators (*IL6*, *PTGS1*). Downregulated genes reflect suppression of the original FHWT identity (*TCF21*, *FBLN5*, *HOXB6*).

**Cross-comparison I — AT vs FHWT relapse/progression:** Near-zero overlap (1 concordant gene, *COL11A1*) confirms that anaplastic transformation is molecularly distinct from both primary and secondary FHWT resistance. The three failure modes activate non-overlapping transcriptional programs.

**Cross-comparison II — Acquired vs de novo anaplasia:** Only 7 genes are shared between the AT recurrent UP set and the de novo DAWT program (out of 172 AT-UP and 94 DAWT-UP). The 7 concordant genes converge on stress-response/AP-1 activation (*FOS*, *EGR1*) and ECM/aggressive-phenotype markers (*SERPINE1*, *MMP9*, *COL10A1*). The 95% non-overlap between acquired and de novo anaplasia indicates that histological anaplasia can arise through largely distinct transcriptional routes, and that therapeutic strategies for de novo DAWT cannot be directly extrapolated to AT tumors.

**Pathway enrichment synthesis (Step 4):** ORA and GSEA across all six gene sets and four databases reveal four major biological themes that organize the Wilms tumor aggressiveness axis:

(1) *De novo DAWT = MYC/E2F/mTOR proliferative drive.* GSEA Step 1 identifies mTORC1 Signaling (NES = −2.11), E2F Targets (−1.98), MYC Targets V1 (−1.85), and G2-M Checkpoint (−1.74) as the dominant DAWT-enriched Hallmarks — a coherent hyper-proliferative cell cycle program uniquely associated with de novo anaplastic histology. FHWT primary tumors are instead enriched for Wnt-beta Catenin Signaling (NES = +1.83), consistent with the CTNNB1-mutant, blastemal-enriched biology of favorable histology disease.

(2) *Acquired anaplasia = comprehensive NF-κB/inflammatory-EMT rewiring.* The AT-specific UP gene set (n=163) shows dominant enrichment for EMT (ORA Hallmarks; padj = 4.9 × 10⁻²²) and TNF-alpha Signaling via NF-κB (padj = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁸), with RELA, NFKB1, and JUN identified as the primary upstream regulators by TRRUST. GSEA Step 3 extends this to a 17-Hallmarks program encompassing inflammatory cytokine signaling, hypoxia, complement, coagulation, KRAS Up, IL-6/JAK/STAT3, and TGF-beta — a broad-spectrum inflammatory rewiring absent from de novo DAWT. EMT is the dominant and most specific single hallmark of the acquired anaplastic state. KRAS Signaling Up in the AT recurrence — juxtaposed with KRAS Signaling Dn enrichment in both FHWT-enriched and DAWT-UP gene sets — marks a striking inversion of KRAS effector output at transformation.

(3) *NF-κB is the convergence point of de novo and acquired anaplasia.* TNF-alpha Signaling via NF-κB is the only major Hallmark enriched in both the DAWT UP ORA and the AT-specific UP ORA/GSEA. The 7 concordant AT∩DAWT genes (FOS, EGR1, SERPINE1, MMP9, COL10A1, STEAP4, SPRR2F) are all NF-κB/AP-1 downstream targets, identifying this node as the molecular intersection of two otherwise independent routes to anaplastic histology.

(4) *The two FHWT resistance modes are biologically orthogonal.* Primary resistance (progression) is pre-defined at diagnosis by a coherent immune program — Allograft Rejection, Complement, Coagulation, and Interferon Alpha/Gamma Response (GSEA Step 2 NES = −2.21 to −2.33; all FDR < 0.001) — with STAT1 and REL as upstream TF regulators. This immune-inflamed but likely immunosuppressive/immune-excluded baseline is specific to primary resistance and absent from secondary resistance. Secondary resistance (relapse) is defined by Wnt-beta Catenin Signaling and Glycolysis, with LEF1 and CTNNB1 as TF regulators — a differentiated, nephron-like program consistent with initial treatment sensitivity followed by late relapse. These two programs are mutually exclusive at both the gene and pathway level, confirming that primary and secondary resistance are pre-determined at diagnosis and activate non-overlapping transcriptional circuits.

**Therapeutic target prioritization (Step 4 extension):** ChEMBL v34 druggability mapping of the AT-specific UP transcriptome identifies two FDA-approved targets (PTGS2/COX-2; IL-6) and five clinical-stage candidates (ABCB1/P-gp, MMP12, AKT3, CD44, CXCL8) within the 163-gene AT signature. The highest translational priority nodes are anti-IL-6 blockade (siltuximab/tocilizumab), COX-2 inhibition (celecoxib), and P-gp inhibition (tariquidar) — three orthogonal mechanisms targeting the inflammatory amplification axis, prostaglandin-NF-κB feedback, and efflux-based chemoresistance respectively. The AHR antagonist BAY 2416964 (Phase 1/2) represents an emerging opportunity. RUNX1 and NR4A1 are AT-specific transcription factor nodes without current clinical agents but with active preclinical programs.

## Therapeutic Target Prioritization: AT-Specific Program

### Rationale

The AT-specific UP transcriptome (n=163 genes, filtered for AT recurrence uniqueness relative to DAWT concordance) defines a set of actionable candidates for therapeutic targeting in relapsed anaplastic Wilms tumor — a setting with no established salvage standard of care beyond UH-Wilms protocols. To prioritize candidates, we queried the ChEMBL database (v34) for approved drugs and clinical candidates against the AT-specific gene products, focusing on the highest-expressed, biologically central nodes identified in the pathway enrichment analysis.

### Results

Ten AT-specific genes with compelling transcriptomic rationale were queried against ChEMBL v34 for mechanism-of-action, approved drugs, and clinical development stage (Supplementary Table S_druggability). The analysis identifies two FDA-approved targets, three phase 2–3 clinical candidates, and two poorly-drugged transcription factor/orphan receptor nodes that are emerging preclinical targets.

**Tier 1 — Approved targets (max_phase = 4):**

*PTGS2 (COX-2)* ranks among the top AT-specific UP DEGs and sits at the intersection of the NF-κB and EMT programs. Multiple COX-2 inhibitors are FDA-approved (celecoxib, diclofenac, nabumetone, sulindac); celecoxib has demonstrated preclinical anti-tumor activity across pediatric solid tumor models and is the most studied in combination with cytotoxic regimens. Prostaglandin E2 produced by COX-2 amplifies NF-κB signaling and promotes EMT via EP receptors, making PTGS2 a mechanistically grounded node at the convergence of the two dominant AT Hallmarks.

*IL-6* is the top AT-specific differentially expressed cytokine (GSEA IL-6/JAK/STAT3 Hallmark NES = 1.93, Step 3) and a major transcriptional output of the NF-κB axis. IL-6 is directly targeted by the approved anti-IL-6 antibody siltuximab (Sylvant) and the anti-IL-6R antibody tocilizumab (Actemra). Both agents have established safety profiles in pediatric inflammatory conditions and have been investigated in oncology. Siltuximab holds the most direct anti-IL-6 mechanism; tocilizumab may be preferred if trans-signaling via soluble IL-6R is implicated.

*AHR (aryl hydrocarbon receptor)* is upregulated in the AT program and activates dioxin-response element (DRE)-driven transcription overlapping with NF-κB at shared enhancers. The only approved AHR-modulating agent (tapinarof) is an agonist for atopic dermatitis; the AT context requires antagonism. AHR antagonists BAY 2416964 (Phase 1/2, NCT05084976) and SRN-005 are in early oncology trials, representing a translational opportunity for AT relapse.

**Tier 2 — Clinical candidates (max_phase 2–3):**

*ABCB1 (MDR1/P-glycoprotein)* is the top single AT-specific UP gene and encodes the canonical multidrug resistance efflux pump. P-glycoprotein mediates resistance to dactinomycin, doxorubicin, and vincristine — the backbone agents of Wilms tumor frontline therapy. ABCB1 upregulation in AT recurrence is mechanistically consistent with the pre-existing ABCC11 nonsense mutation observed in patient PAJNGH (primary tumor), suggesting a coordinated ABC transporter-axis switch toward chemoresistance. P-gp inhibitors tariquidar (Phase 3) and elacridar have demonstrated reversal of MDR1-mediated resistance in preclinical models.

*MMP12* is upregulated in the AT program and contributes to ECM remodeling supporting mesenchymal transition and tumor invasion. AZD1236 (Phase 2/3, AstraZeneca) selectively inhibits MMP9/12 with nanomolar IC50 values. Repurposing rationale in solid tumor EMT is supported by the dominant EMT signature of AT (Hallmark NES = 1.77).

*CD44* is a classical EMT/cancer stem cell marker and surface receptor for hyaluronan upregulated in the AT program. RG7356 (Phase 2, NCT01358903) and bivatuzumab represent the most advanced clinical agents. The CD44⁺ cancer stem cell phenotype associated with mesenchymal transition may enrich for the therapy-resistant cellular subpopulation that survives initial Wilms tumor treatment and drives AT recurrence.

*CXCL8 (IL-8)* is a downstream NF-κB effector and top AT UP gene driving neutrophil recruitment and CXCR1/CXCR2-mediated autocrine EMT signaling. BMS-986253 (anti-IL-8 antibody; Phase 2, NCT03400332) is in clinical trials in combination with nivolumab. CXCL8 contributes to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment component of the AT program.

*AKT3* is upregulated in the AT program and represents the predominantly expressed AKT isoform in renal tissue. AKT3-selective inhibitor M2698 (Merck; Phase 2) is more isoform-selective than pan-AKT inhibitors and may reduce off-target metabolic toxicity relevant in pediatric patients.

**Tier 3 — Poorly drugged emerging targets:**

*RUNX1* and *NR4A1* are AT-specific transcription factors with no approved or late-stage clinical mechanism in ChEMBL. RUNX1 is a top upstream regulator in TRRUST analysis and intersects both the NF-κB regulon and the EMT program. Small molecule inhibitors (Ro5-3335, AI-4-88) are in preclinical development. NR4A1 (Nur77) is an orphan nuclear receptor with ligand-binding capacity; cytosporone B and DIM-C-pPhOH derivatives can induce its pro-apoptotic nuclear export.

### PPI Network Topology and Community Structure

To understand how the AT-specific genes are organized at the protein interaction level and identify structurally important targets beyond simple connectivity, we constructed a STRING PPI network (confidence ≥ 700, functional network, human taxon 9606) for the 163 AT-specific UP genes and applied Louvain community detection to the resulting graph.

Of 163 input genes, 78 formed a connected network (177 edges). Louvain partitioning identified 10 communities with a modularity coefficient Q = 0.474, indicating strong modular organization. Four major modules account for 56 of the 78 connected nodes and correspond to distinct biological programs:

*Module 1 — NF-κB / Cytokine core (n = 19):* The dominant module comprises the core inflammatory output of the NF-κB axis: IL6, CXCL1/2/3/8, CCL2/20, IL1B/IL1RN, IL15, PTGS1/2, TNFAIP6, TLR5, NFKBIZ, and CSF2RB. This module recapitulates the ORA/GSEA finding that TNF/NF-κB signaling is the most enriched pathway of the AT program (ORA padj = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁸; GSEA NES = 1.93). The high intra-module connectivity reflects co-regulation by NF-κB/RELA at shared enhancers rather than direct biochemical interactions — these genes are coordinately activated transcriptional outputs, not a signaling cascade.

*Module 2 — ECM / Matrix remodeling (n = 15):* FN1, MMP12, COL11A1/COL12A1/COL5A3, COMP, CTHRC1, CTSK, FAP, POSTN, THBS2, TIMP1, SERPINA1, DCSTAMP, and SPP1. This module represents the structural and proteolytic remodeling arm of the AT EMT program, providing the permissive extracellular environment for mesenchymal invasion.

*Module 3 — EMT / Adhesion (n = 14):* CD44, SNAI1, TWIST1, ICAM1, MUC1, PTPRC, LAMA3/LAMB3 (laminin chains), KRT7, AREG, PRRX1, MSX1, PGR, and CD48. This module contains the canonical EMT transcription factors (SNAI1, TWIST1) and cell adhesion/surface markers, defining the mesenchymal identity shift that characterizes AT recurrence.

*Module 4 — AP-1 / Stress TF (n = 8):* EGR2/3/4, JUNB, NR4A1, ZFP36, AKT3, BCL2A1. This module comprises immediate-early transcription factors and stress-response regulators that constitute a secondary transcriptional relay downstream of the primary cytokine signal.

**SPP1 as the inter-module structural bottleneck.** Analysis of betweenness centrality — the fraction of shortest paths in the network passing through each node — identifies SPP1 (osteopontin) as the structural bottleneck of the entire network, with betweenness = 0.043 (highest) despite only 10 direct interactions (rank 9 by degree). This topology places SPP1 at the interface of Modules 1, 2, and 3: it is formally assigned to the ECM module but bridges the cytokine and EMT modules through two independent receptor systems — RGD-dependent binding to integrins αvβ3 and αvβ5 (connecting to ECM mechanics and signalling) and CD44 ligation (connecting to the EMT/CSC identity module). Removing SPP1 from the network would disconnect the cytokine module from the EMT module more effectively than removing any other single node.

The flat, near-clique topology of Module 1 (the cytokine cluster) has direct therapeutic implications: because these genes are co-regulated outputs of a shared upstream driver (NF-κB/RELA), they exhibit high redundancy — inhibiting one cytokine is compensated by the others. Single-cytokine blockade (anti-CXCL8, anti-CCL2) is therefore expected to have limited network-level impact. More effective strategies address the network at three levels: (i) the shared upstream regulator (IL-6/IL-6R blockade as the highest-activity arm, effectively suppressing downstream STAT3 amplification); (ii) the structural inter-module bottleneck (SPP1, the only node whose removal disrupts cross-module communication); and (iii) the EMT arm directly (SNAI1/TWIST1 pathway or their surface readouts CD44/MUC1).

**TF regulon analysis: direct NF-κB targets in the AT program.** To distinguish direct transcriptional targets of NF-κB from genes upregulated through indirect or paracrine mechanisms, we cross-referenced the AT-specific UP gene set against the TRRUST v2 curated TF–target database using the GSEA leading-edge gene lists for RELA, NFKB1, JUN, and STAT3. Of 165 AT-specific UP genes, 26 (16%) are direct RELA and/or NFKB1 transcriptional targets. These partition into a canonical core (10 genes regulated by RELA+NFKB1+JUN including *IL6*, *CXCL8*, *PTGS2*, *ABCB1*, *IL1B*, *ICAM1*) and a canonical-only tier (13 genes regulated by RELA+NFKB1 but not JUN, including *SNAI1*, *FN1*, *BCL2A1*, *CCL20*, *S100A6*, *TNFSF10*). Four genes — *SPP1*, *CTSK*, *MMP19*, *DCSTAMP* — are regulated exclusively by JUN/AP-1 with no NF-κB input, which structurally distinguishes SPP1 from the cytokine cluster and positions it as an AP-1-driven ECM node. Three further genes (*MUC1*, *CHI3L1*, *ZFP36*) are unique STAT3 targets not shared with the NF-κB regulon.

Two findings from this analysis extend the therapeutic target landscape. First, NF-κB directly transcribes both master EMT regulators in the AT program: *SNAI1* is a direct RELA+NFKB1 target (LFC = 1.15, padj = 4.1 × 10⁻³) and *TWIST1* is a direct RELA+JUN+STAT3 target (LFC = 1.39, padj = 4.0 × 10⁻³). This means the connection between the NF-κB cytokine module (C1) and the EMT module (C3) observed in the PPI network is not solely mediated through paracrine cytokine signalling: NF-κB wires the EMT program transcriptionally and directly. Targeting NF-κB upstream (e.g., IKK inhibition or IL-6R blockade) therefore simultaneously suppresses cytokine production and EMT TF expression. Second, *BCL2A1* (Bfl-1/A1; LFC = 2.37) is a direct RELA+NFKB1 target encoding a BCL2-family pro-survival protein with minimal sensitivity to approved BH3 mimetics (venetoclax, navitoclax). BCL2A1 upregulation adds a third apoptosis resistance layer to the AT program alongside ABCB1/MDR1 efflux and S100A10-mediated membrane anti-apoptosis, all three independently driven by NF-κB. The TF regulon membership for all 33 identified genes is provided in Supplementary Table S_tf_regulon.

### Community 6: S100 / Annexin Module — Apoptosis Resistance and the Niclosamide Opportunity

The S100/Annexin module (C6; n=7: *ANXA1*, *S100A4*, *S100A6*, *S100A10*, *S100A16*, *ANXA2R*, *HSH2D*) represents a functional cluster of calcium-sensing scaffolding proteins that co-upregulate in the AT recurrence program. Within this module, two members have distinct therapeutic relevance to the cancer cell death phenotype: S100A10 as a direct apoptosis suppressor and S100A4 as an EMT/invasion amplifier acting through the RAGE receptor.

**S100A10 as a pro-survival node.** S100A10 (p11) is a calcium-binding scaffolding protein that forms a heterotetrameric complex with Annexin A2 (ANXA2) on the inner plasma membrane leaflet. The S100A10/ANXA2 complex serves two pro-survival functions: first, it anchors tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and urokinase (uPA) at the cell surface, driving local plasminogen → plasmin conversion and subsequent MMP-mediated matrix degradation that protects against anoikis; second, it suppresses intrinsic apoptosis by stabilising anti-apoptotic components at the membrane. Loss of S100A10 across multiple cancer models consistently sensitises cells to chemotherapy- and TRAIL-induced apoptosis, and S100A10 expression is elevated in drug-resistant cell lines. In the AT transcriptome, S100A10 upregulation appears alongside the dominant chemoresistance gene *ABCB1*/MDR1 — together these may constitute a dual-mechanism resistance barrier combining efflux-based drug exclusion (ABCB1) with apoptosis threshold elevation (S100A10).

**The niclosamide repurposing rationale.** Niclosamide is an FDA-approved anthelminthic drug with a well-characterized safety profile but poor oral bioavailability. In cancer, niclosamide has been shown to transcriptionally suppress S100A10, inhibit Wnt/β-catenin signalling (by promoting LRP6 endocytosis), and block STAT3 phosphorylation — three mechanisms that are each individually relevant to the AT resistance program (Wnt/β-catenin enrichment at FHWT relapse; STAT3 as the downstream effector of IL-6/JAK signalling at AT recurrence; S100A10-mediated apoptosis resistance). This convergence of mechanisms on a single repurposed agent is unusual and gives niclosamide a multi-target profile within the AT molecular network. Preclinical activity has been demonstrated in Wilms tumor cell lines (WT-CLS1, SK-NEP-1) and in a range of pediatric solid tumors including Ewing's sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma. Niclosamide is currently in early oncology repurposing trials for colorectal cancer (NCT02532114) and pediatric refractory AML (NCT05188170). No Wilms tumor-specific trial has been conducted.

**S100A4 and the RAGE axis.** S100A4 (Metastasin/MTS1) is a well-validated pro-metastatic and pro-invasive protein acting through two mechanisms: extracellularly, secreted S100A4 binds RAGE (Receptor for Advanced Glycation End-products, encoded by *AGER*), activating NF-κB and upregulating MMPs in recipient cells — a paracrine amplification loop that connects C6 to the cytokine core (C1) and ECM module (C2). Intracellularly, S100A4 binds non-muscle myosin IIA, disrupting cytoskeletal organisation and promoting amoeboid motility. The RAGE receptor has been clinically explored as a drug target in neurodegeneration: the small-molecule antagonist azeliragon (TTP488) completed Phase 3 (NCT02080364) for Alzheimer's disease, demonstrating safety and blood-brain barrier penetrance. FPS-ZM1 blocks RAGE-mediated S100A4 signalling in invasion models. Pentamidine, an FDA-approved antiprotozoal, disrupts the S100A4-myosin IIA interaction at low micromolar concentrations in preclinical models and represents a second repurposing opportunity. Importantly, RAGE is also activated by multiple DAMPs and alarmins released from the inflammatory tumor microenvironment generated by Module 1, suggesting that RAGE blockade could attenuate both the S100A4-driven invasion arm and the broader paracrine NF-κB amplification loop simultaneously.

**Biological boundaries of the C6 targeting strategy.** ANXA1, the highest-betweenness member of C6 (betweenness = 0.024, bridging C6 to the cytokine module), warrants caution as a direct target. ANXA1 functions as an endogenous anti-inflammatory mediator acting on FPR1/FPR2 receptors — its upregulation in the AT program likely reflects a homeostatic feedback response attempting to dampen the inflammatory storm generated by Module 1, rather than an active driver of tumor progression. Inhibiting ANXA1 in this context risks amplifying the NF-κB/cytokine axis rather than suppressing tumor survival. Therapeutically, the C6 module is best approached through S100A10 (apoptosis sensitisation) and S100A4/RAGE (anti-invasion / NF-κB attenuation), with ANXA1 left intact as a potential endogenous brake.

### Prioritization Summary

Translational priority is highest for three strategies applicable within the pediatric oncology setting: (1) anti-IL-6/IL-6R blockade (siltuximab or tocilizumab) to suppress the dominant JAK/STAT3 inflammatory axis at the apex of the cytokine module; (2) COX-2 inhibition (celecoxib) to reduce prostaglandin E2-mediated NF-κB amplification and EMT priming from within the cytokine core; and (3) ABCB1/P-gp inhibition (tariquidar co-administration) to restore chemosensitivity to frontline cytotoxics at relapse. These three interventions target orthogonal mechanisms — inflammatory cytokine signaling, prostanoid amplification, and efflux-based chemoresistance — and are not mutually exclusive. Network topology analysis adds a fourth structural priority: **SPP1/osteopontin blockade** as the highest betweenness bottleneck node and the only gene whose removal computationally disrupts communication between all three major functional modules.
